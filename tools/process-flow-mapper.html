What this tool actually does

It treats the process as a chain of steps where work leaves each step by one of its exits, in the proportions you gave. From that it works out how many times a unit of work passes through each step on average, and multiplies by the times you entered.

  • Every headline figure is an average per unit of work. Lead time is the mean. Half of the work, or far more than half, can take longer. The figures under "How long it takes" come from walking simulated units through the same list, and rest on the same inputs.
  • A wait is an input. The tool does not model queues. If a step waits two days in your list it waits two days here, whether one request a week arrives or a thousand.
  • Percentages are taken as fixed and independent. A request sent back for the third time is given the same chance of passing as one seen for the first time. Real rework usually is not like that.
  • Exits that do not add up to 100% are scaled, and exits with no percentage share what is left evenly. Each case is listed under "Check these" with the reading used. If the reading is wrong, every figure downstream is wrong and looks no different.
  • Rework is chosen by typing order. The exit whose line closes a loop, or leads back to an earlier phase, is the one counted as rework. That decides first-pass yield and the rework figure. The exits counted are listed and drawn dashed.

Not modelled, at all

  • Work done at the same time, beyond the simple case. Branches written with => must come back together at one step, with no way in or out of a branch in between, and the work goes on when the slowest arrives. A branch that can be abandoned, a join that needs only some of the branches, and people shared between branches are not represented. The headline lead time through a split is the slowest branch on average, which is a little low when the branches vary.
  • Capacity is a ceiling, not a queue. With a staff line the tool divides the hours available by the work each unit needs. That says what a lane could carry if work arrived perfectly evenly and nobody was ever idle or absent. Real limits are lower, and a lane well under 100% can still have a long queue.
  • Queues, batching and priorities. Waiting that grows with load, work released in batches, expediting and work that jumps the queue are all outside the model.
  • Variation you did not type. In the simulation a time given as one number never varies, a range is even between its ends or a triangle around its most likely value, and every draw is independent of every other. Real waits move together and have longer tails. The figures are not a worst case and not a confidence interval.
  • Calendars. A day is the number of working hours you set and a week the number of days you set, for every lane alike. Shifts, holidays, time zones and a lane that works different hours are not represented.
  • Whether the map is the real process. The tool checks that the list is well formed. It cannot know about the step everyone does and nobody wrote down.
  • Cost, quality and risk. Only time and counts.

The usual error is in the waits. People remember how long a job takes and badly underestimate how long it sat. A lead time built from guessed waits is a guess with decimals.

What the picture can mislead about

  • The tint is relative. The darkest step is the largest on this map, not large in any absolute sense. A map where nothing matters still has a darkest step.
  • Position means nothing. Columns follow the order of steps, not time. Distance across the page is not duration.
  • Lane colour. The palette has eight colours and repeats after that.
  • Exports. A PNG carries the page font for Latin text only, and an SVG uses whatever fonts the program opening it has. Check an export before it goes into a report.

Never use this for

  • A delivery date, service level or turnaround promised to a customer, a regulator or a court.
  • Deciding how many people to hire, lay off, or schedule.
  • Clinical, emergency, safety-critical or life-safety response times.
  • A compliance, audit or validation record of how a process is performed.
  • A business case, contract or penalty clause without an independent analysis behind it.

Before you rely on a result

Measure the waits instead of estimating them. Check the percentages against counts from real work. Walk the map with the people who do the job. Have someone qualified in the process review the result before it supports a decision, a commitment or a report.

Syntax
Sales: Enter order -> Check credit
Sales does "Enter order", and the work then goes to "Check credit".
Sales: Enter order {10 min, wait 2 h}
Ten minutes of work, after two hours of waiting to be started. Units are s, min, h, d and wk. Either part can be left out.
Sales: Enter order {5-15 min, wait 1-2-8 h}
A time that varies. Two numbers are the lowest and the highest. Three put the most likely in the middle. The averages use the middle of the range, and the spread under the headline uses all of it.
Finance: Credit OK? -> yes 85%: Pick goods, no 15%: Tell customer
Two exits with a label and a share each. A name ending in ? is drawn as a decision.
Warehouse: Pack order -> {wait 1 d}: Deliver
A wait on the way to the next step, such as transit. It goes before the colon, after any label and share: yes 85% {2 h}: Pick goods.
Sales: Take order => Pick goods, Raise invoice
A double arrow means all of these happen, at the same time. The branches must meet again at one step, which goes on when the slowest arrives. Steps on a faster branch are shown with their slack.
Customer: (Order placed)
Brackets mark a start or an end. A step named only as a target needs a line like this to say which lane does it.
== Fulfil ==
Starts a phase. Steps below it are grouped, and the phase gets its own share of the lead time.
in: Order form from Customer
out: Invoice to Customer
What the phase above takes in and from whom, and what it hands on and to whom. Several are separated by commas. They fill the SIPOC table and change no figure.
lanes: Customer, Sales, Finance
The order of the lanes, top to bottom. Without it they appear in the order first typed.
arrivals: 30 / wk
How much work arrives. Adds hours of work per week to each lane.
staff: Sales 2, Finance 1 @ 25%
People on duty in a lane during working hours, and the share of their time this process gets. Gives the most the process can carry and the lane that limits it, and with arrivals, how busy each lane is.
@ Credit OK?: limit is 30 days overdue
A note kept with that step and shown when you hover over it.
: Finance #4a3aa7
A colour for that lane.
// comment
Ignored, as is a line starting with #.

Rows copied from a spreadsheet can be pasted straight in: columns are lane, step, touch time, wait, next. Several exits go in the last cell separated by semicolons, such as yes 80%: Ship; no 20%: Fix.

An exit that leads back to a step already passed is rework. Which exit of a loop counts as the return is decided by typing order: the line that closes the loop. A name containing a comma, a colon or an arrow goes in double quotes. A lane cannot be called lanes, arrivals, staff, in or out.

Working hours, which is what d and wk mean in your list and how results are turned back into days. One shift is 8, two are 16, round the clock is 24. "Wait 2 d" on an 8 hour day is 16 working hours.

Down the page keeps a long process narrow enough to read without scrolling sideways. Exit labels are shortened to fit over their own column.
