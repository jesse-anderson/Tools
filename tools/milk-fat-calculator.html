What this tool actually does

  • It adds up fat by weight. Each ingredient's weight times its fat percent gives its fat in grams; the mix's fat percent is total fat over total weight.
  • Volumes become weight through USDA cup weights. A cup of whole milk weighs 244 g and a cup of heavy cream 238 g. A fat percent between the presets gets a density in between; above heavy cream's 36.08% the heavy cream density is held.
  • A label gives a range, not a value. Nutrition Facts round fat to the nearest half gram or gram, so a small serving of cream can stand for a wide span of fat percents. The tool shows that span.
  • Total volume is the sum of the parts. Whether dairy volumes add exactly when mixed was not checked.

Not checked, at all

  • Your product. Brands, seasons and herds differ. Preset values describe USDA's samples, not your carton, and label values are rounded.
  • Temperature. USDA states none for its cup weights, and density changes with temperature.
  • Other standards. A legal name such as half-and-half or heavy cream also depends on ingredients, processing and other limits in 21 CFR 131. The tool checks only the fat range, and nothing outside the United States.
  • Food safety. Nothing here concerns pasteurization, storage or how long a mix keeps.

A result inside the half-and-half range means the arithmetic lands there. It does not make a product half-and-half.

Never use this for

  • A Nutrition Facts panel, a fat claim or any product label.
  • Deciding whether a product meets a standard of identity, a contract or a regulator's test.
  • Infant feeding, medical diets or anything where a fat error could harm someone.
  • Commercial formulation without a lab analysis of the actual ingredients.

Before you rely on a result

Use the fat figure from your own carton, weigh rather than measure by volume when it matters, and for anything sold or labeled, have the product tested by a qualified laboratory.

How to use
  1. Mix. Pick a product for each ingredient, then its amount and unit. The fat percent fills in from USDA; change it to your carton's figure if you know it.
  2. From a label. Choose "Nutrition label" and copy the panel: total fat in grams and the serving size. Use the serving's mL or g when the label prints one, since a label cup is 240 mL and a kitchen cup is 236.6 mL.
  3. Reach a target. In the second section, say what you have, what you will add, and the fat percent you want. Or ask for a batch of a set size and get the amount of each product.

Fat percent on dairy is by weight. Mixing equal volumes of two products does not give the plain average of their percents, because cream weighs less than milk; the difference is small in most kitchen mixes.
