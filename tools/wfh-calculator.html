What this tool actually does

  • It multiplies daily costs by days. An office day and a home day each have a cost you describe. The yearly figure is those costs times the number of each kind of day, plus the yearly items you enter.
  • Commute time is turned into money only at the rate you choose. The money total without time value is always shown beside the total with it.
  • Tax is a 2026 outline, not a return. By default each salary is taxed with the 2026 federal brackets and standard deduction, Social Security and Medicare, and one flat state and local rate you enter, so a raise or a pay gap is taxed at each rate it passes through. With "A rate I enter", your one rate is applied to each change in pay instead.
  • Cost of living is one ratio. Between two places, what is left after tax and working costs is scaled by BEA's 2024 price levels for the two places. It is a national statistical average for each place, not your rent or your basket.
  • Published figures are national averages. The IRS mileage rate, AAA's costs per mile and gas price, and the Census commute time describe the average driver, not you.

Not checked, at all

  • Your actual tax. Itemized deductions, credits, phase-outs, the alternative minimum tax, state brackets and local rules are not modeled. A spouse's Social Security is not tracked, and payroll tax is taken on pay before every pre-tax deduction, which overstates it slightly when the deductions are health premiums.
  • Tax rules. The notes on commuting, home office and pre-tax commuter benefits are a US federal outline from 2025 and 2026 publications. Rules differ by state and country and change every year.
  • Pay beyond salary. Health insurance, retirement match, equity, bonus, paid leave and job security are not compared unless you enter them yourself as other pay.
  • Prices over time. Gas, fares, food and energy are held constant for the year.
  • Things with no agreed price. Promotion and career effects, productivity, health, isolation and mentoring are listed on the page and left out of every number.

A large saving tells you your inputs add up to a large saving. It does not tell you a job is the better job.

Never use this for

  • Preparing a tax return, claiming a deduction, or setting up a commuter benefit election.
  • Payroll, expense reimbursement, mileage claims or any figure an employer or the IRS will rely on.
  • A salary negotiation figure presented to anyone as fact.
  • Legal decisions about employment terms, relocation or child custody arrangements.

How to use

Two minutes: enter your pay and tax rate, describe your trip in, and read the cost of one office day on the right. Or load an example to see a filled-in scenario.

  1. Pay and schedule. Salary or hourly pay, hours, your filing status and your state's income tax rate. The tool works out federal and payroll tax from the 2026 tables and shows the rate on your next dollar; switch to "A rate I enter" to use your own. Then the office days your job asks for.
  2. Getting there. One or more legs for the trip in, such as drive to a station then take a train, with the door to door minutes. Untick "Same trip home" if the trip home differs. Fuel cost is distance ÷ mpg × price: 20 miles at 25 mpg and $3.50 a gallon is $2.80.
  3. Food, care and extras. What an office day costs that a home day does not: bought lunch, coffee, after-school care, a dog walker, dry cleaning. Lunch and coffee at home go in the Home side section, so only the difference counts.
  4. Home side. What a home day adds: energy, a coworking day now and then, a desk and chair spread over their life.
  5. Time. How to value an hour of commuting, and where the regained hours would go. Family time is counted in hours; a dollar value for it is optional and kept out of the totals.

The chart and table cover every pattern from full office to fully remote. A monthly pass or parking permit is used only where it is cheaper than paying per day, so the bars do not fall in a straight line. Tick "Compare a second job" to put two offers side by side.
